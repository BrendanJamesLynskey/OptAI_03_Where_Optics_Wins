01

A Caution to Start

McMahon's review of the physics of optical computing argues that “the speed of light is not a key differentiating physical property of optics for computing”, and sets out eleven features of optics (parallelism, passive linear operations, low-loss data movement and others) from which an advantage could be built, if a design uses several of them and avoids the costs of conversion and precision (Nat. Rev. Phys. 5, 717, 2023; arXiv:2308.00088).

  • So the question is never “is light fast?” but whether, for a given workload, the energy saved inside the optics exceeds the energy spent converting into and out of it, holding weights in it, and keeping it busy.
  • The Fourier Optics series asked that question for Fourier engines in LLM inference and training and found the answer mostly negative (Fourier Optics for Inference; for Training). This deck asks it for general optical matrix multiplication, which covers far more of the work.

05

Memory and Amdahl

  • Data movement dominates digital AI energy. At 45 nm an 8-bit multiply costs 0.2 pJ, a 64-bit SRAM access 10 pJ and a 64-bit DRAM access 1.3–2.6 nJ (Horowitz, ISSCC 2014). Making the MAC cheaper changes little if the weights still arrive from DRAM.
  • So optics must hold its weights, in phase-change cells or programmed phases, for long enough to use them many times: a weight-stationary design. Model sizes far exceed what one optical array holds, so large models are tiled, and every reload is a conversion per weight (the interactive's reload setting).
  • Amdahl's law applies at system level: if a fraction f of the energy is optical MACs and they become free, total energy falls by at most f. With memory, nonlinearities and control left digital, f is well below 1 (optical share and the Amdahl bound).
  • Architecture models say the same: Andrulis et al. find conversion and DRAM can take a significant portion of a photonic accelerator's energy (arXiv:2405.07266); the classic survey of where accelerator energy goes is Sze et al. (Proc. IEEE 105, 2295, 2017).
06

Precision for Inference and for Training

Inference

  • INT8 and FP8 inference is standard; matching INT8's error needs about 8 ENOB (the noise rule).
  • Some tasks tolerate far less: about 90% digit accuracy at about 0.66 photons per multiplication (Wang et al., Nature Commun. 13, 123, 2022).
  • Precision can vary by layer (Garg et al., arXiv:2102.06365).

Training

  • Gradients span a wide dynamic range and need more precision than activations (gradient precision).
  • Weights change every step, so an optical array would be rewritten each step (mask rewrites per step).
  • Analogue noise in training acts partly as regularisation, partly as error (analogue noise in a training loop).

Inference is the easier target: lower precision, static weights and a forward pass only. Most of what the companies in deck 04 describe is inference.

07

The Scaling Argument: 1/d

Anderson et al. studied Transformer inference with optical matrix multiplication and modelled how its energy scales with model size. Their key finding is that “optical energy per multiply-accumulate (MAC) scales as 1/d where d is the Transformer width”: wider models share each conversion and each photon among more MACs (arXiv:2302.10360; TMLR 2024).

What the paper projectsAgainst
About 100× energy advantage for some of the largest current Transformers (about 500 billion parameters)Digital processors at 300 fJ/MAC
More than 8,000× for models of a quadrillion parametersThe same
  • These are modelled projections, not measurements, and they rest on assumptions about converters, memory access and precision that the paper states; a further >100,000× case assumes cheaper memory access, better conversion and 4-bit precision.
  • The argument is the reuse argument of OptAI 02, slide 01 taken to model scale: if an optical array were as wide as the model, conversions per MAC would fall as 1/d. The practical limit is how wide an array can be built (OptAI 02, slide 09), and whether weights can be held in it.

  • Where latency is worth paying for: iterative solvers that loop many times through one matrix (Ising and combinatorial optimisation), control loops, signal processing on data that arrives fast, and decisions that must be taken within a clock cycle or two.
  • Large-model serving is rarely in this class: its latency is dominated by memory bandwidth and batching, not by the time light takes to cross a chip (LLM Inference Simulators).
09

When the Data Is Already Light

  • Imaging and sensing skip the input conversion. A scene arrives as light, so a diffractive or photonic front-end processes it before any detector and need only digitise a compressed result. ACCEL reports 4.6 peta-operations per second, over 99% of them optical, 74.8 peta-operations per second per watt and 72 ns per frame on vision tasks (Chen et al., Nature 623, 48, 2023; the authors' figures).
  • Data arriving over fibre can be computed on before detection: Netcast multiplied at 40 aJ per multiply at an edge device whose weights arrived over 86 km of deployed fibre (Sludds et al., arXiv:2203.05466; Science, 2022). Compute on data in a link is the subject of Compute in Transit.
  • Why this matters: removing the input DAC removes half of the conversion bill of OptAI 02, slide 07, and the scene's own pixels give an enormous N for free.
10

Transforms, Convolutions and Encryption

  • Fourier transforms and convolutions are what a lens computes natively. For LLMs the Fourier Optics series found the transform share of prefill FLOPs small, even in FFT-mixing models (FOptInf 02), and the simulated optical prefill pools broke even only under favourable assumptions (FOptInf 03, the break-even point).
  • Convolutional vision networks are a better fit: convolution is the bulk of their work and their weights are small and static (Xu et al.'s 11 TOPS convolutional accelerator, OptAI 01, slide 08).
  • Fully homomorphic encryption spends much of its time in number-theoretic transforms, the finite-field cousin of the FFT; optical engines for it are analysed in FHESim 04, Optical NTT Engines, where exact arithmetic makes the precision demands severe. A company working on this is in deck 04.

12

What to Take Away

  • Speed of light is not the advantage; shared conversions, passive linear operations and parallelism are, when a workload can use them.
  • Optics wins at large N and low precision with static weights, low static power and high utilisation; the interactive shows how fast the winning region shrinks when any of these slips.
  • Memory decides: a DRAM access costs thousands of MACs, so an optical engine must hold its weights; for large models that means tiling and reloads.
  • Inference before training; weight × activation before attention.
  • The scaling argument (energy per MAC as 1/d) projects 100× for today's largest Transformers and far more beyond, as a model, not a measurement.
  • The clearest niches: data that is already light (vision, sensing, fibre), latency-bound iterative solvers, and convolution-heavy networks.
